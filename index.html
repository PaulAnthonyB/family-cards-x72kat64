Core principles

Card 1 · The operating system

Don't ask. Observe and act.

"Tell me what you need" is another task on her pile. Hand her the plate. Take the baby. Start the shower. If you'd have to ask her to ask — just do it.

Mum's care = the whole family's care

Her sleep, recovery, and mental health are the load-bearing wall. Protecting them is strategy, not selfishness.

Oxygen mask first

  • Mum: one solid sleep chunk per night. Physio. Outside. Breaks. Non-negotiable.
  • Dad: one solid sleep chunk per night. 30 min movement every 1–2 days.

Don't go down with the ship

If it's a one-person job → then the other parent sleeps. No guilt. No "keeping company."

Protect earplug time

Off-duty parent is fully off. On-duty handles everything short of emergency.

Circuit breaker: "Same team"

Either of us can say it, any time. Response = hand squeeze, not words. Stop. Reset. We're not opponents.

The relationship is the foundation

None of this — the new family of four — works without us. The Mum/Dad relationship is the ground everything else stands on. Nurture and protect it. A 5-minute "us" check-in daily (not logistics, not the kids — us). A hand on the back while brushing teeth. Eye contact across the room. Small, often, deliberate.

Imperfect > pleasing

  • Stop people-pleasing. We don't owe anyone a "yes" right now. Energy is finite; spend it on us four.
  • Stop perfecting. Getting it wrong and adjusting is the path. Mitigating every failure mode in advance is its own failure mode.
  • It's okay to ask. It's okay to hear no. It's okay to say no. Three separate permissions. All true.

The drowning spiral

Card 2 · Right now

If we're both exhausted, both drowning, both guilty about self-care → then this is a predictable pattern, not a relationship problem.

What's happening

Running on empty makes self-care look like theft from the other. Guilt refuses rest. Refusing rest deepens the deficit. Depletion looks like resentment but is fuel starvation.

The resentment is a symptom — not evidence about your partner.

The move

One of us sleeps now. Coin flip if needed. The non-sleeper doesn't earn anything by suffering through. We will swap.

The script

"We're both depleted. That's the problem. Who sleeps first?"

Mum — early warning

Card 3 · The most important card

Last time, this is where it broke. Catching the slide at week 2 is 10× easier than climbing out at week 6.

Daily check-in (Dad asks, every evening)

  • Sleep chunk?
  • Outside today?
  • Out of 10?

A number is easier than a sentence when she's depleted. Three days under 5 = act, don't wait.

Don't wait for her to ask

She can't. Noticing → naming → asking is three tasks she doesn't have the bandwidth for. Observe and act.

Her specific tells

  • Physical/emotional disengagement. Not wanting to be hugged or touched. She is normally very affectionate — withdrawal from touch is a strong, reliable tell.
  • Over-apologising. Apologies for things that don't warrant them.
  • Slow response, or unresponsive. Going quiet, going internal, going flat.
  • Overwhelmed by tiny things. A small task or ask producing a disproportionate reaction.

Red flags — call today

  • Thoughts of harm to self or baby
  • Feeling disconnected from baby ("this doesn't feel like my baby")
  • Cannot sleep 48+ hrs even with opportunity
  • Hopelessness that doesn't lift

Action ladder

  • Extra sleep chunk + outside + a meal she didn't cook (24–48 hrs)
  • Call in help for a half-day off
  • GP appointment. PANDA: 1300 726 306
  • Maternal & Child Health 24/7: 13 22 29

Dad's job

Mum will minimise. PND lies to the person who has it. Trust the pattern, not her in-the-moment assessment. Three tells + "I'm fine" = book the GP. She agreed to this when she was clear-eyed. Honour past-her.

"If we see the tells, we act without asking permission, because past-us decided present-us couldn't be trusted to."

The next 72 hours

Card 4 · Read in week one

The cesarean meds are wearing off over the next 1–3 days. Expect a pain step-up, and a mood dip around days 3–5 ("baby blues" — milk coming in, hormones crashing).

This is biology, not a sign things are going wrong. Naming it now means recognising it then.

Specifically watch for

  • Mum trying to do too much as pain ramps up (she'll underestimate it because right now feels manageable)
  • Day 3–5 tearfulness — normal, but check it doesn't stay past day 10–14
  • You crashing at 48–72 hrs once adrenaline fades — your turn for a sleep chunk is coming. Claim it.

Two things to do this week

  • Book Mum's 6-week GP check-in now. Default = appointment exists. Cancel if not needed.
  • Brief one outside person (a parent, a close friend) — their job is to ask Mum honestly at weeks 2, 4, 6 and push past "I'm good!"

The toddler

Card 5 · When the regression hits

Bub #2 changes bub #1's world more than it changes yours. Their regression is grief, not behaviour. Discipline misses the point; connection is the move.

Expect, and don't be alarmed by

  • Sleep regression, new clinginess, separation anxiety
  • Hitting, biting, lashing out (sometimes at the baby)
  • Toilet-training backslide
  • Food refusal, meltdowns over nothing
  • "Baby talk," wanting to be carried, regressing to younger behaviours

This is grief and demotion, not naughtiness. Their entire social order just changed. Meet it with connection, not consequences.

The non-negotiables

  • 15 minutes of 1:1 "special time" daily. Phone down. Baby elsewhere. Toddler picks the activity.
  • Don't blame the baby out loud. Not "I can't, I'm feeding the baby" → instead "I'll be there in 2 minutes."
  • Let them "help." Fetch the nappy. Sing to bub. Choose the onesie. Inclusion beats exclusion.
  • Pre-warn transitions. "After this story, it's bath." Their world is unpredictable right now — narration is medicine.
  • Protect the routine. Daycare days, nap times, bedtime. Known-good anchors in an unknown week.
  • Tag-team bedtime. Whoever has more in the tank does it tonight. Don't default it to one person.

When they lash out at the baby

Calm, not angry. Move bub to safety, then get low and connect with the toddler. "You're having big feelings. The baby is little and we keep her safe. Come here." The behaviour is a flare, not a fight.

Watch for

The toddler going too quiet, too compliant. Big kids who suddenly stop demanding attention are sometimes the ones who need it most.

Dad — early warning

Card 6 · When Dad is sliding

Paternal PND is real and under-diagnosed. Dad runs the early-warning system for Mum; this card runs it for him. Catching it early matters for the same reasons.

Dad's specific tells

  • Arms folded low across the belly, thumbs massaging the inner elbows. His body's "I'm exposed" signal. It surfaces under guilt and self-loathing before he's consciously aware of it.
  • Slow to respond, or not responding at all. Going quiet, going internal, going flat.
  • Apologising profusely — apologies for things that don't warrant them, or stacked apologies for the same thing.
  • Self-punishment / self-harm urges. Punishing self-talk, refusing rest or food as penance, any urge toward physical self-harm. This is a red flag on its own — straight to step 1.

For Mum

Read this if you want, but not as a job. If you happen to notice the tells, it's useful information — but this is not your job. Your only assignment is your own oxygen mask. If you spot it and have capacity, a hand on the back and "same team" is enough. Dad is responsible for noticing himself.

Action ladder

  • Name it out loud to Mum (the hardest step and the most important one)
  • Sleep chunk + outside + movement, within 24 hrs
  • Talk to a mate — a real one, not a surface one
  • GP. Beyond Blue: 1300 22 4636. MensLine: 1300 78 99 78
  • If self-harm urges aren't shifting: Lifeline 13 11 14, or present to ED.
"If Dad catches himself three times in a day, or Mum points it out, or any self-punishment urge surfaces — he names it out loud and starts the ladder. No 'I'm fine.' No 'I just need to push through.'"
